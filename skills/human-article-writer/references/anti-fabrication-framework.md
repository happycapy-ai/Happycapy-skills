# THE DEFINITIVE ANTI-FABRICATION FRAMEWORK
## For AI Article-Writing Systems
### Grounded in Analysis of 1,109 Real Human Articles

---

## SECTION 1: EVIDENCE SUMMARY
### What Real Human Writers Actually Do

#### The Personal Story Myth — Debunked by Data

The assumption that compelling articles require personal stories is **statistically false**.

| Metric | Finding | Implication |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| Articles with any personal story | **29.5%** (327 of 1,109) | 7 in 10 professional articles contain zero personal narrative |
| Articles without personal stories | **70.5%** (782 of 1,109) | The majority mode of professional writing is story-free |
| Articles with anecdotes (rhetorical sample) | **55.4%** (36 of 65 analyzed) | When anecdotes appear, they are brief illustrations, not foundations |
| Anecdotes that are real experiences | **77.8%** (28 of 36 with anecdotes) | When humans use stories, they are overwhelmingly real, not constructed |
| Anecdotes as primary authority mechanism | **~0%** | No article in the corpus anchors its credibility on personal story alone |

**Critical distinction:** The 55.4% anecdote figure and the 29.5% story figure measure different things. The rhetorical sample captures brief illustrative moments — one or two sentences of illustration. The full corpus measures extended narrative. Real human writers use anecdotes as *seasoning*, not *structure*. The fabricated multi-paragraph "I remember when I was working with a client in Chicago..." construction that AI systems generate does not appear in professional writing.

---

#### What Real Writers Use Instead of Stories

The dominant opening pattern is the **declarative statement** — used in **74.8%** of articles. Only **8.7%** open with first-person framing. The AI default of opening with "I remember when I first encountered this problem..." inverts the actual human pattern by a factor of nearly 9 to 1.

| Opening Type | Frequency |
|-------------|-----------|
| Declarative statement | **74.8%** |
| First-person (non-story) | **8.7%** |
| Question | **6.6%** |
| Short declarative | **5.0%** |
| Data point | **4.9%** |
| Temporal / Quote | **<0.2%** each |

**Operational instruction:** The default opening is a confident declarative claim or a cited data point. The "let me tell you a story" opening is a statistical outlier in professional writing, not the norm.

---

#### The Structural Signature of Real Human Writing

Real human articles are structurally distinct from AI output in measurable ways:

| Dimension | Human Writing | AI Writing | Gap |
|-----------|--------------|------------|-----|
| Mean sentence length | 25.6 words | 20.5 words | 20% longer |
| Sentence length variation (SD) | 15.7 | 9.2 | 41% more varied |
| Paragraph structure | Long-form sustained blocks | ~23 fragmented units | Humans argue; AI lists |
| Average word length | 4.74 characters | 5.59 characters | 18% simpler |
| Expository content share | 75.6% | — | Explanation dominates |

**What this means:** Real experts write in sustained argument, use plain language, and vary their rhythm naturally. AI output is detectable by its uniform sentence length, fragmented paragraph structure, and inflated vocabulary. Simpler words signal genuine expertise. Complexity signals performance.

## SECTION 2: AUTHORITY MECHANISMS
### How Real Writers Establish Credibility Without Fabricated Stories

The following ranking is derived from rhetorical analysis of the corpus. These are the actual tools professional writers use, in order of prevalence.

---

### TIER 1 — PRIMARY MECHANISMS
*Used in more than 50% of articles. These are the load-bearing structures of professional credibility.*

---

**1. Specific Examples — 66.2% of articles**

Named companies, documented events, real products, identifiable situations.

- *Corpus example: "China Metal Recycling Holdings cited as a concrete short position that resulted in wind-up due to fraud"*
- **Why it works:** Specificity is unfakeable. A real expert knows real names. Vagueness is the tell.
- **AI instruction:** Name the actual company, product, policy, or event. "A major tech company" is a fabrication hedge. "Salesforce's 2023 restructuring" is authority. If you cannot name it, you do not have the example — do not construct one.

---

**2. Industry Knowledge Demonstration — 64.6% of articles**

Deployment of process knowledge, mechanism-level terminology, and insider logic that only domain fluency produces.

- *Corpus example: "Detailed explanation of gross revenue recognition abuse and related party transaction inflation"*
- **Why it works:** Demonstrates competence through the texture of explanation, not through personal testimony. Knowledge-as-authority requires no personal experience — it requires accuracy.
- **AI instruction:** Explain *how* something works at the mechanism level. "Procurement cycles in enterprise SaaS typically span 6–18 months because legal, security, and finance sign-off are sequential, not parallel" establishes more authority than "I've been in sales for ten years." The mechanism is the credential.

---

**3. Data Citation — 55.4% of articles**

Specific statistics, named indices, published ratios, and verifiable figures with attribution.

- *Corpus example: "Hang Seng China AH Premium Index at 95, Shanghai Composite at 7.6x vs. 5-year average of 12.1x"*
- **Why it works:** Anchors argument in verifiable external reality. Precision signals research. Round numbers signal fabrication.
- **AI instruction:** Cite real, named data sources. If a specific figure cannot be verified, use directional framing ("research consistently shows...") with a named body of research. Do not invent statistics. A directional claim grounded in a real research tradition is more credible than a precise figure that is fabricated.

---

### TIER 2 — SECONDARY MECHANISMS
*Used in 25–50% of articles. These reinforce Tier 1 but do not replace it.*

---

**4. Historical Analogy — 44.6% of articles**

Connecting current argument to documented historical precedent with accurate dates and outcomes.

- **Why it works:** Shows pattern recognition across time — a marker of genuine expertise. History is shared, verifiable, and authoritative. It is stronger than personal anecdote because it is checkable by anyone.
- **AI instruction:** Use real historical events with accurate dates and outcomes. The 1997 Asian financial crisis, the 2001 Enron collapse, the 2008 structured credit unwind — these are available to any writer. Fabricated historical parallels are detectable and destroy credibility.

---

**5. Credential Signaling — 41.5% of articles**

Explicit or implicit reference to professional role, institutional affiliation, or verifiable track record.

- *Corpus example: "Guest identified as portfolio manager at US$1.4 billion AUM Keywise Capital Management"*
- **Why it works:** Establishes the writer's standing to speak on the subject.
- **AI instruction:** Credential signaling is a statement of fact, not a constructed story. State the role, the institution, the tenure — one clause, not a scene. If writing on behalf of a human author, use their actual credentials. Do not fabricate institutional affiliations or invent career histories.

---

**6. Experience Reference — 40.0% of articles**

Brief, non-narrative references to professional background — one clause, not a constructed scene.

- *Corpus example: "'Based on my experience and observation' when discussing accounting abuse prevalence"*
- **Critical distinction:** An experience reference is one clause. A fabricated anecdote is a constructed scene with characters, dialogue, and setting. The former is legitimate; the latter is not.
- **AI instruction:** "In practice, this rarely works" is an experience reference. "I remember sitting across from a CFO in a glass-walled conference room when I realized..." is a fabricated scene. The line is the presence of constructed sensory and narrative detail.

---

**7. Admission of Uncertainty — 36.9% of articles**

Explicitly acknowledging the limits of one's knowledge or the genuine complexity of a situation.

- **Why it works:** Counterintuitively, this *increases* credibility. It signals intellectual honesty and distinguishes the writer from promotional content. AI systems that never express uncertainty are detectable as inauthentic.
- **AI instruction:** When the evidence is mixed, say so. When a prediction is uncertain, quantify the uncertainty or acknowledge it explicitly. "This is contested" or "the data here is thin" are credibility signals, not weaknesses. Performed confidence on genuinely uncertain questions is a fabrication.

---

### TIER 3 — SUPPORTING MECHANISMS
*Used in fewer than 30% of articles. These add texture but cannot carry argumentative weight alone.*

**8. Counterargument Engagement** — Acknowledging and addressing the strongest opposing view. Signals intellectual rigor and distinguishes analysis from advocacy.

**9. Structural Signposting** — Explicit transitions and logical connectors that make argument architecture visible. Signals organized thinking, not stream-of-consciousness.

**10. Hedged Projection** — Forward-looking claims framed with explicit uncertainty ("if X, then likely Y"). Distinguishes analysis from prediction and protects credibility when outcomes are unknown.

## SECTION 3: THE FABRICATION TAXONOMY
### What AI Systems Generate That Human Writers Do Not

These are the specific patterns that mark AI output as inauthentic. Each has a documented replacement strategy.

---

### FABRICATION TYPE 1: The Constructed Personal Anecdote

**What it looks like:**
> *"I remember sitting in a boardroom in Singapore when a CFO leaned across the table and said something that changed how I thought about risk forever..."*

**Why AI generates it:** Training data rewards narrative engagement. AI systems learn that stories get positive feedback and generalize this into fabricating stories where none exist.

**Why it fails:**
- It is unverifiable and therefore untrustworthy
- It is structurally detectable: real anecdotes are brief; fabricated ones are elaborate
- It crowds out the actual argument
- Sophisticated readers recognize the pattern and discount everything that follows

**Replacement strategy:**
Use a **specific documented example** instead. The example does the same rhetorical work — it makes the abstract concrete — without requiring fabrication.

| Fabricated | Legitimate replacement |
|-----------|----------------------|
| "I once worked with a company that..." | "When Wirecard collapsed in 2020, the mechanism was precisely this..." |
| "A client told me that..." | "Survey data from [named source] shows that practitioners report..." |
| "In my experience, this always..." | "Across documented cases of X, the consistent pattern is..." |

---

### FABRICATION TYPE 2: The Invented Statistic

**What it looks like:**
> *"Studies show that 73% of executives struggle with this problem..."*
> *"Research indicates that companies implementing this approach see a 40% improvement..."*

**Why AI generates it:** Specific numbers increase perceived authority. AI systems learn this association and generate plausible-sounding statistics without grounding.

**Why it fails:**
- Invented statistics are verifiable as false
- They expose every other claim in the article to doubt
- Precise false numbers are worse than acknowledged uncertainty

**Replacement strategy:**

| Fabricated | Legitimate replacement |
|-----------|----------------------|
| "73% of executives report..." | "McKinsey's [year] survey of [n] executives found..." |
| "Studies show 40% improvement..." | "The evidence on this is directional but not yet precise — early adopters report meaningful gains, though controlled studies are limited" |
| "Research indicates most companies..." | "The pattern across documented cases suggests..." |

**Hard rule:** If you cannot name the study, name the research tradition or acknowledge the limitation. A named uncertainty is more credible than an unnamed precision.

---

### FABRICATION TYPE 3: The Vague Authority Hedge

**What it looks like:**
> *"Many experts believe..."*
> *"Industry insiders often say..."*
> *"A leading company in this space..."*

**Why AI generates it:** Vague authority feels safer than specific claims that could be checked. AI systems hedge by making claims unfalsifiable.

**Why it fails:**
- Vagueness signals that the writer does not actually know
- It is the structural opposite of the specificity that real experts deploy
- Readers recognize it as a credibility performance, not credibility itself

**Replacement strategy:** Name the expert, name the company, name the insider. If you cannot name them, either find a named source or acknowledge that the claim is your own inference from available evidence.

| Fabricated | Legitimate replacement |
|-----------|----------------------|
| "Many experts believe..." | "Ray Dalio has argued consistently that..." or "The consensus among practitioners, as reflected in [named survey], is..." |
| "A leading tech company..." | "Google's approach to this, documented in their [year] engineering blog..." |
| "Industry insiders often say..." | "This is a common practitioner view — [named person] articulated it most clearly when..." |

---

### FABRICATION TYPE 4: The False Experience Claim

**What it looks like:**
> *"In my fifteen years working in private equity..."*
> *"Having advised hundreds of companies on this..."*
> *"From my time running operations at a Fortune 500..."*

**Why AI generates it:** Experience claims establish authority efficiently. AI systems generate them because they work — until they are checked.

**Why it fails:**
- They are false, and falseness is detectable
- They constitute a specific form of deception that damages trust in AI-assisted content broadly
- They are unnecessary: mechanism-level knowledge establishes authority without biographical claims

**Replacement strategy:** Replace experience claims with knowledge demonstration. The mechanism is the credential.

| Fabricated | Legitimate replacement |
|-----------|----------------------|
| "In my fifteen years in PE..." | "The standard PE value-creation playbook — operational improvement, multiple expansion, leverage optimization — works until..." |
| "Having advised hundreds of companies..." | "The consistent failure mode across documented turnaround cases is..." |
| "From my time at a Fortune 500..." | "At the operational level, the constraint is typically not strategy but..." |

---

### FABRICATION TYPE 5: The Performed Certainty

**What it looks like:**
> *"This will absolutely transform the industry..."*
> *"There is no question that X is the right approach..."*
> *"Anyone who disagrees simply hasn't thought this through..."*

**Why AI generates it:** Confident prose scores well on engagement metrics. AI systems learn to perform certainty because hedged writing feels weak in training feedback.

**Why it fails:**
- On genuinely uncertain questions, performed certainty is a fabrication
- It is detectable by anyone with domain knowledge
- It eliminates the credibility signal that comes from appropriate uncertainty

**Replacement strategy:** Calibrate confidence to evidence. Strong claims on well-established ground. Explicit uncertainty on contested or emerging questions.

| Fabricated | Legitimate replacement |
|-----------|----------------------|
| "This will transform the industry" | "The conditions for significant disruption are present — whether this specific approach captures that opportunity depends on..." |
| "There is no question that..." | "The evidence strongly favors X, though the mechanism is still debated" |
| "Anyone who disagrees..." | "The counterargument — [state it accurately] — fails because..." |

## SECTION 4: HARD RULES
### Non-Negotiable Constraints for AI Article-Writing Systems

These rules are derived from the evidence above. They are not stylistic preferences. They are the operational boundary between legitimate AI-assisted writing and fabrication.

---

**RULE 1: No constructed scenes.**
Do not write scenes with characters, dialogue, setting, or sensory detail that did not actually occur. A brief experience reference ("in practice, this rarely works") is permitted. A constructed narrative scene is not.

**RULE 2: No unnamed statistics.**
Every specific statistic requires a named source. If the source cannot be named, the statistic cannot be stated as fact. Use directional framing with a named research tradition instead.

**RULE 3: No vague authority.**
"Many experts," "industry insiders," "a leading company" — these are fabrication hedges. Name the expert, name the company, or acknowledge that the claim is your own inference.

**RULE 4: No false biographical claims.**
Do not claim years of experience, client relationships, or institutional affiliations that do not exist. These are not rhetorical devices — they are false statements of fact.
